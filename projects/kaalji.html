02 · Healthcare Research · Strategy · Product

KAALJI

From care need to care delivered.

An independent investigation into a deceptively simple question: when India trains more caregivers, how much of that potential capacity becomes care that families can actually find, use and trust? KAALJI emerged from the evidence as a proposed decision-support concept for tracing that journey without asking the data to prove more than it can.

01 · The question

I did not start with a product idea.

I began with an ageing-population question. My clinical work had made one thing tangible: treatment may end inside a hospital, but care continues after the patient leaves — often inside a family that has to make the system work in real life.

My first question was whether India would have enough caregivers as the population ages. Once I started following the evidence, that question became too crude. The more useful question was what happens between care being needed and care actually being delivered.

I did not build KAALJI and then look for a problem. The evidence changed the problem I thought I was solving.

02 · Evidence changed the question

The hard part was not finding numbers. It was deciding what each number could legitimately mean.

I compared evidence on functional limitations, caregiver training and emerging caregiver-registration systems. None of those measures is inherently weak. The problem appears when a measure from one stage of the system is used as evidence for another — for example, treating training as active workforce supply or functional need as paid-care demand.

03 · Care-capacity model

Training a caregiver is not the same as creating usable care capacity.

Once I stopped treating workforce supply as a single number, the problem became easier to reason about. I mapped care as a conversion pathway: each stage can break for a different reason, and each requires different evidence.

Care need→Family capacity→Formal support→Training→Registration→Employment→Availability→Affordability→Utilization→Retention + quality
01

Need ≠ paid-care demand

Functional difficulty can indicate support needs without proving demand for professional care.

02

Training ≠ active workforce

A completed training programme is one stage. Employment and active availability need separate measurement.

03

Visibility ≠ utilization

A registered caregiver can be discoverable without evidence that care was delivered.

04

Availability ≠ reliable care

Access, affordability, competence, continuity and accountability still shape whether care works for families.

04 · Product direction

The product idea came later: a tool for reasoning before intervening.

At this point, designing another caregiver marketplace would have been premature. The more interesting product problem was upstream: helping programme, healthcare strategy and planning teams understand what kind of care-capacity problem they are looking at before choosing an intervention. That became KAALJI.

KAALJI

A proposed decision-support tool that keeps the evidence, its limits and the next investigative question in the same view — so a team can distinguish a training problem from a workforce-conversion, access, affordability or measurement problem.

05 · Product workflow

A useful product should make uncertainty visible, not tidy it away.

The workflow begins with the decision question, then works backward through the evidence. The aim is not to produce a confident answer at any cost. It is to show what is comparable, what is known, what is missing and what evidence would most improve the next decision.

Question→Evidence→Compatibility→Signal→Evidence strength→Known / unknown→Next investigation

06 · Responsible AI

AI can help navigate the evidence. It should not quietly redefine it.

In KAALJI, AI is useful for language-heavy work such as finding, summarizing and explaining evidence. Comparability checks, calculations, provenance and evidence labels should remain inspectable, while consequential decisions stay with people.

Language + exploration

Where AI can help

Find and summarize relevant evidence, explain source-supported signals, surface candidate hypotheses and support natural-language exploration.

Evidence rules

What should stay explicit

Source provenance, compatibility checks, calculations and evidence labels should remain visible, reproducible and open to challenge.

Accountability

What people still own

Interpretation, programme choices, funding, policy and any clinical or patient-level decisions remain accountable to people.

Prototype boundary

What I am not claiming

This portfolio prototype demonstrates product logic. It is not a production AI system, automated national-data pipeline or autonomous decision engine.

07 · Validation

The next question is not whether the prototype looks convincing. It is whether it improves a real decision.

I would validate KAALJI in four places: whether the evidence is fit for the question, whether the workflow diagnoses the problem sensibly, whether users understand the evidence better, and whether that changes what they do next.

Evidence

Is the question answerable?

Can the available evidence support the question — or at least show honestly where the boundary of knowledge is?

Diagnosis

Does the workflow point somewhere useful?

Does it narrow the plausible explanations and identify a sensible next investigation?

Interpretation

Do people understand the evidence better?

Do source context, limitations and known/unknown reduce unsupported conclusions?

Decision

Does anything change?

Does the product improve what someone does next enough to justify building further?

09 · What changed my mind

I started by asking how many caregivers India might need. I ended up studying what has to happen for one trained caregiver to become dependable care for one family.

The shift sounds small, but it changed the project. Instead of treating ageing as a forecasting exercise, I began looking at the transitions between need, workforce creation, access and actual care delivery.

The numbers were not wrong. The mistake would have been treating them as answers to questions they were never collected to answer.
