# India 2050 — The Future of Care

**Independent Healthcare Strategy & Analytics Project**  
**By Dr. Vedanti Shah**

## The question

India is ageing rapidly. At the same time, India is expanding its caregiver-training pipeline.

But one question is easy to miss:

> **Does training more caregivers automatically create more usable care capacity?**

This project investigates what happens between **care need** and **reliable care delivery**.

The working hypothesis is:

> **India may be expanding its caregiver-training pipeline faster than it is building the economic and institutional mechanisms required to convert trained people into reliable care capacity.**

This is a hypothesis—not a national finding.

## Why this matters

Care is often discussed as either an ageing problem or a workforce problem.

The system is more complicated.

An older adult may have a functional limitation but rely on family support. A trained caregiver may exist but not be employed. An employed caregiver may not be available in the required geography. A service may be available but unaffordable. A worker may enter the sector but leave because the economics are unsustainable.

So the relevant question is not simply:

**“How many people need care?”**

or

**“How many caregivers have been trained?”**

It is:

> **Where is care capacity being lost between need and delivery?**

## What the evidence shows

### 1. Functional need is substantial

LASI Wave 1 reports that among adults aged 60+:

- **23.8%** had at least one ADL limitation.
- **48.0%** had at least one IADL limitation.

These figures describe **functional need**, not paid-care demand.

### 2. Household structure matters

Using LASI household-structure categories, the combined **alone + spouse-only** share among adults aged 60+ is:

- Tamil Nadu: **40.6%**
- Telangana: **41.7%**
- Maharashtra: **22.4%**
- Gujarat: **24.8%**

This is a **proxy for household structure**, not proof that family support is unavailable.

### 3. The caregiver pipeline is expanding

Government data reports **36,785 trainees** through PM-SPECIAL across 32 institutes in FY2023–24.

A derived calculation from the reported state figures shows that Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra together represented:

**25,400 / 36,785 ≈ 69.1%**

This is a calculation from programme data—not an official geographic demand ranking.

The Union Budget 2026–27 subsequently announced a target of **1.5 lakh caregivers** to be trained in the coming years.

## The analytical insight

Training is only the beginning.

The project therefore models care-capacity conversion as:

**TRAINED → REGISTERED → EMPLOYED → AVAILABLE → UTILIZED → RETAINED**

At each stage, capacity can be lost.

For example:

- Training without employment does not create active supply.
- Employment without geographic availability may not solve local shortages.
- Availability without affordability may not become utilization.
- Utilization without retention may produce unstable service capacity.

A national conversion rate cannot currently be claimed from the available evidence. The funnel is therefore a **proposed measurement framework**, not a measured national funnel.

## The strategic opportunity

### Care Capacity Intelligence

The proposed opportunity is **not another caregiver marketplace**.

It is a decision-intelligence layer for understanding where care capacity is breaking down.

The model brings together:

**CARE NEED** → Functional and support requirements  
**FAMILY CAPACITY** → Living arrangements and informal support  
**WORKFORCE CAPACITY** → Training → registration → employment → availability  
**CARE ECONOMICS** → Affordability + worker sustainability  
**UTILIZATION** → Conversion of available capacity into delivered care  
**QUALITY** → Reliability, standards and outcomes

The core decision question becomes:

> **Where is care capacity breaking down—and what intervention should we investigate?**

## What makes this a product-thinking project

The project moves through a product/strategy chain:

**Problem framing → Evidence gathering → Data interpretation → Gap identification → Hypothesis formation → Decision model → Potential intelligence layer**

The proposed interface is intended to help decision-makers investigate patterns such as:

- high care need + weak family capacity
- adequate training + poor employment conversion
- available workforce + low utilization
- adequate supply + poor retention
- fragmented data preventing confident decisions

These are **proposed diagnostic patterns**, not measured classifications.

## What is still unknown

A strong strategy analysis should make its unknowns visible.

The current evidence does **not** establish:

- a national paid-care conversion rate
- current caregiver availability after training
- a validated national paid-care market size
- causal effects of household structure on paid-care utilization
- a defensible state-by-state intervention ranking

These are precisely the areas where better integrated data could create additional decision value.

Primary sources include:

Where figures are calculated from source data, they are explicitly labelled **derived**.

Where relationships are proposed for further testing, they are labelled **hypothesis**, **proxy**, or **proposed model**.

## Important limitation

This is an **independent healthcare strategy and data-analysis project**, not a peer-reviewed academic study.

It does not claim to represent the views of the cited institutions.

The objective is not to manufacture certainty where the data is incomplete.

The objective is to show how a healthcare problem can be taken from:

**a broad societal question → evidence → structured analysis → explicit uncertainty → a testable strategic opportunity.**
